*FDVU is the Norwegian term for facility management, operations, maintenance and development (forvaltning, drift, vedlikehold og utvikling). NS 3451 is the Norwegian standard that classifies building elements and technical systems.*

In [an earlier article](/articles/what-do-the-people-who-use-fdv-documentation-say/) I asked the people who use FDVU documentation every day how it works for them. The answer was clear: everyone had come across documentation that was outdated, wrong or impossible to find, and they spent on average more than an hour a week searching. In [another](/articles/why-digitalising-fdvu-pays-off/) I looked at what that costs. This article is about the third question: what do you do about it?

Since spring 2026 I have been working on getting the FDVU documentation for Boligbygg's properties into one shared structure based on NS 3451:2022. As of October, 406 buildings have been pulled out for structuring, and just over half are done. Along the way I have become convinced of one thing: structure is not tidying up. Structure is what decides whether documentation lives or dies.

## Documentation dies quietly

Documentation rarely disappears in one big event. It dies quietly, in small jobs.

A ventilation fan is replaced. The service technician leaves a new O&M binder, or emails a PDF. The person who receives it is busy and puts it wherever is quickest: on the desktop, in a folder called "New", or in the FDVU system under the building but not under the system it belongs to. The old documentation stays where it was. Now there are two versions, and neither is marked as current.

A year later the fan stops in the middle of the night. The operations technician opens the FDVU system, finds the old documentation first, calls the wrong supplier and orders the wrong part. It is not a dramatic failure. It is an hour here and two hours there, and it is exactly what the people in the survey described: documentation in many different places, under the wrong name or on the wrong system, and drawings that aren't updated after refurbishments.

Notice what went wrong. The documentation *existed*. It had even been delivered. What was missing was a place where it obviously belonged, and a rule for what happens to the old one. Without those, every single filing is a small decision someone has to make alone, and everyone makes it their own way. Every way makes sense on its own. The sum is a structure nobody can explain.

## Without structure, documentation is disposable

This is the real cost of missing structure: documentation gets made once, for one purpose, and then it decays.

A construction project delivers a complete FDVU package at handover. That is the best day of the documentation's life. From that day on only one thing happens: the building changes, and the documentation doesn't. Every replacement, every refurbishment, every new service agreement is a point where the documentation either gets updated or starts to lie. Without structure, nobody knows where the update goes, so it doesn't go in.

The result is that after a few years the documentation is as good as no documentation, only more expensive. It still takes up space, it still has to be searched through, and it gives a false sense of security. When someone finally needs it, the drawing has to be redrawn and the condition survey redone, because nobody trusts what is there.

That is why I don't think of structure as a clean-up project. A clean-up puts the documentation in order once. Structure is what keeps it in order without anyone having to clean up again.

## Why NS 3451

The first choice was which structure. We could have made our own, but a home-made classification is always logical to the people who made it and baffling to the people who come after. We needed a structure that wasn't *ours*.

NS 3451:2022, the Norwegian table of building elements and system codes, is the classification the industry already uses: in design, in cost estimates, in condition surveys under NS 3424, and in the FDVU systems themselves. Building, HVAC, electrical power, telecoms and automation, each with its own sub-levels, so the ventilation system always belongs under 36 and not under "technical", "HVAC – misc" or the supplier's name.

When the documentation follows the same classification as everything else, three things happen:

- A contractor can deliver straight into the structure, because they already sort their own deliverables by building element.
- An operations technician knows where a manual belongs without asking, and finds it again in three years.
- A new colleague recognises the structure from their last job. Knowledge moves with people instead of away from them.

That is the point of a standard. It removes the small decision from every filing, because the answer to "where does this go?" has been given in advance.

## What "done" means

The second most important thing we did was decide what a fully structured building is. Without that definition, "done" is a feeling and progress is a guess.

A building counts as done when

- every document is linked to the right building and the right building element under NS 3451,
- documents are named so the title says what it is, which system it concerns and which version applies,
- outdated versions are removed or archived, so only one current version exists, and
- the documents that operations and maintenance cannot do without are either in place or clearly marked as missing.

The last point matters. A building where we *know* the fire safety concept is missing is in better shape than a building where we *think* it is in there somewhere. Structure is also about making the gaps visible.

With this list, every building is checked against the same measure, and the number in the curve means the same in October as it did in May.

The work runs in three tracks at once, and all three are about keeping the documentation alive, not just tidying it.

**1. Structure and quality.** Building by building, we go through what is in the FDVU system, link documents to the right building element, correct names and remove duplicates. This is the clean-up, and it is necessary, but it is the least important part. It puts the documentation in order once.

**2. Shared practice.** A revised FDVU manual describes how documentation is to be delivered, named and entered, from projects and from day-to-day operations alike. It is used in orders and contracts, so documentation arrives in the right form from the start instead of being cleaned up afterwards. This is what stops the next refurbishment from undoing what we just put in order.

**3. Competence.** Structure doesn't help if the people using the system keep filing things the old way. So we walk through the structure with operations staff and project managers, and spot-check what gets entered. The goal is not that everyone knows NS 3451 by heart, but that everyone knows where to find the answer to "where does this go?".

The curve above shows the pace picking up through the year. Not because we work harder. The first buildings were spent making the rules: the definition of "done", the naming standard, what to do with documents that don't fit. Once the rules are settled, each new building goes faster, and it is the rules, not the clean-up, that keep the buildings done.

## What is hardest

**Documents that don't fit anywhere.** A condition report covers the whole building. A service agreement covers twenty properties. The answer is not a category called "misc", because that is where documentation goes to die. The answer is a clear rule: documentation that concerns the whole building sits at building level; documentation that concerns several buildings sits in one place and is linked from the others. The rule can be almost anything, as long as it is the same every time.

**Old documentation of doubtful value.** Is a twenty-year-old drawing that may not be accurate better than no drawing? Usually yes, but only if it is marked as old. Documentation without a date and a version is more dangerous than missing documentation, because it looks right.

**Keeping the structure once it is made.** This is the biggest risk, and it was the clearest finding in the survey: it is the small jobs afterwards that kill documentation. So the most important routine we have is not a clean-up day but a question asked every time something is replaced: *which document is now outdated, and what replaces it?*

## In short

- Documentation doesn't die because it's missing. It dies because it sits in the wrong place, under the wrong name, in the wrong version, and because nobody knows where the update goes.
- Use a structure the industry already knows. NS 3451 means the documentation can be read by everyone, not just by the people who made it, and removes the small decision that is otherwise made differently every time.
- Define what "done" means before you start counting. Otherwise you are measuring a feeling.
- A clean-up puts documentation in order once. Structure, a manual and routines are what keep it alive.
