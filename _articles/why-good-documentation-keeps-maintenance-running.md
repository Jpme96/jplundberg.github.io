Most people think of maintenance as tools, spare parts and people on the floor. In my experience, the work that keeps a system reliable starts much earlier, with the documents nobody notices until they are missing.

As a Maintenance Operation and Management engineer, I split my time between planning technical work and keeping records in order. Over time I've learned that the two can't really be separated. A maintenance plan is only as good as the information it's built on.

## The cost of missing information

When a component fails, the first questions are always the same: when was it last serviced, what was done, and which version of the procedure was used? If the answers are scattered across emails, old spreadsheets and someone's memory, a one-hour job can easily turn into a full day.

> Reliable equipment depends on reliable records.

## What good documentation looks like

It doesn't have to be complicated. The systems that work best tend to share a few simple habits:

- **One source of truth.** Everyone works from the same, current version of each procedure.
- **Clear ownership.** Every document has someone responsible for keeping it up to date.
- **Records written at the time.** Notes are logged when the job is done, not reconstructed a week later.
- **Easy to find.** If it takes longer to locate a document than to do the job, people will skip it.

## Small routines, long-term results

Document control can feel like admin for admin's sake, but it pays off every time an audit goes smoothly, a new colleague gets up to speed quickly, or a recurring fault is spotted early because the history was there to see it.

My goal is always the same: build systems that are dependable enough that nothing slips through the cracks, and simple enough that people actually use them.
